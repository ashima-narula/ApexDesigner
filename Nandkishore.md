# Historical Cases: Training Failures in Complex Systems

My individual topic was historical cases where training issues have caused the system to fail. I researched three cases that show the link between training, teamwork, and design.

---

## The London Ambulance Failure

In 1992, the LAS launched an automated dispatch system with almost no staff training. When the software faced real world stress, it began making errors and eventually got locked. Because the interface was complex and staff were untrained, they could not troubleshoot the mistakes which lead to massive delays and preventable deaths.

Key Finding: The system was designed to help operators to make decisions, but it was so complex that when it failed, the staff didn't know how to fix it. No training was provided to the operators before the first launch date. Later training was inadequate and irrelevant because the software was constantly changing which resulted in this incident [7].

---

## The Queensland Health Payroll Failure

The Queensland Government replaced its payroll system with a new digital version in 2010. However, the system rollout was so rushed that the staff did not receive enough training to understand how it worked. When it launched, thousands of healthcare workers were paid incorrectly or not at all. This created a massive administrative disaster that cost over $1.2 billion to fix and took years to resolve.

Key Finding: Staff were expected to use a highly technical system without understanding its basic functions, leading to thousands of manual errors. Without any ongoing support, the small mistakes made by untrained staff cumulatively made the entire system unmanageable. Since the transition was not handled properly and training was missing, the healthcare workers lost trust in the new technology [8].

---

## The Addenbrooke Hospital case

In 2014, the Addenbroke hospital in the UK spent £200m on a new digital record system. However, the system was so complicated that the staff's performance dropped by 20% overnight. Even though the doctors were trained, the software required so many steps and clicks that it became a distraction. The system was so difficult to navigate that it eventually led to a critical incident because the staff couldn't care for patients and troubleshoot the software at the same time.

Key Finding: A system that is technically perfect but takes too long to use will be rejected by professionals. In healthcare, if a design is too slow, it stops being a helpful tool and becomes a distraction that puts patients at risk. The increased cognitive overload of the system could not be overcome by increased training [9].

---

## Conclusion

From these case studies, the following conclusions have been identified:

Systems which are designed to take over human decision making cannot be managed if staff cannot troubleshoot them during a crisis.

Training cannot fix a system that is fundamentally too hard for a human to use under real-world stress.

Without ongoing support and clear communication, small mistakes by untrained staff lead to a total loss of trust in new technology.

A technically perfect system will be rejected by professionals if it takes too long to use or requires too many steps and causes cognitive overload.
